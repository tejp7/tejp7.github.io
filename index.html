How LLMs are trained: why AI sometimes makes things up

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) can write answers that sound confident and polished, even when they are wrong. In this AI Interaction, I worked through an interactive lesson on how LLMs are trained, and learned firsthand why they sometimes "hallucinate," producing answers that sound real but have nothing behind them.

Description

The activity was a guided, hands-on conversation with an AI tutor. Instead of just reading about training, I answered questions, made predictions and sorted tasks, then got feedback on my reasoning. It covered the four things that shape what a model is good or bad at: its training data, its post-training, the context it is given, and the tools it can use.

I completed the lesson and earned the badge "Knows Why the Robot Sometimes Makes Stuff Up."

Objective

Understand how LLMs are trained, and use that understanding to decide when to trust a smooth-sounding AI answer and when to ask for a source, a tool or a human expert.

Process
  1. Experienced a hallucination firsthandAsked to name the president of Velmora, I typed "Arman Delacroix" without thinking. The name sounded real but had nothing behind it, which is exactly what an LLM does when it fills a gap with a plausible guess.
  2. Learned how pre-training worksModels are first trained to predict the next word. They produce plausible continuations based on patterns they've learned, not checked facts.
  3. Learned what post-training addsExtra training teaches models to follow instructions better, but it doesn't make them fact-check themselves automatically.
  4. Judged training-data coverageEstimated how much training data exists for different topics. I judged that live stock prices are "basically none" in training data, and learned that some languages have very little data.
  5. Saw the role of context and toolsLearned that a model can't know live information, such as today's weather, unless it is given the context or a tool to look it up.
  6. Sorted tasks by riskPlaced tasks into what AI does well and a "Danger Zone." I put exact multiplication in the Danger Zone because it needs a calculator, not a good guess.

Where AI fits, based on how it's trained

Good uses

Writing drafts, explaining common topics, and brainstorming, where patterns from lots of training data help.

Danger Zone

Live information, exact math, and legal advice, where a plausible guess isn't good enough without a source, tool or expert.

The result
Badge unlocked: Knows Why the Robot Sometimes Makes Stuff Up

The tutor's completion summary confirmed I understood the main idea: LLMs produce plausible continuations from learned patterns, not checked facts, and what they are good at depends on training data, post-training, context and tools.

My takeaway

"Next time I use AI, I'm going to ask myself where the answer actually comes from."

Value proposition
Unique value

Artifacts 1 and 2 show what I can build and explain with AI. This artifact shows that I understand why models behave the way they do, including their limits. Knowing where an answer comes from lets me decide when AI can be trusted and when it needs a source, a tool or a person to check it.

Relevance

Hallucinations are one of the biggest risks for teams putting AI into real products. Engineers who understand training data, post-training, context and tools can design features that look up facts instead of guessing, which supports my goal of building AI that people can trust.
